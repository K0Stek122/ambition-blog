Money is a translation of value.
A lot of people think that the key to success is to have a lot of money. To make a lot and to spend it on whatever you want. They think that money = freedom. But there's an art here that many people miss: the art of personal finance management. Not many people can do it. But if you live in a first-world country, you should be able to survive off of the minimum wage in that country.

There are exceptions to this, of course. Life happens and you may trip up, but by following one simple rule you can make those slip-ups less painful. Here's what you do:

**You invest in my new crypto coin.**

Nah, just kidding. Here's the rule that I learned from the book "The Richest Man in Babylon": for every income you get, no matter if it's from full-time, your business, freelancing, a lottery ticket, a gift, a rented house—of that money you always save up at least 1/10 of that income. If you make £2,000 a month you take out £200 minimum. If you can do more than do more, but 1/10 is the absolute minimum. You put that money away in a box or a savings account, and you never ever spend it. When the time comes, you invest it. It doesn't have to be stocks. A business, a house, a business venture, a short-term opportunity.

If you start this venture yourself, you only invest in ventures that you know about. If you invest in a friend, invest in topics that their friend has knowledge about. If your friend wants to invest in a hamster breeder, then even if they are a world-renowned mechanical engineer, they definitely do not know squat about breeding hamsters.

That's the essence of money.

## Managing Expectations and Minimum Wage

But let us get back to managing your expectations. What should the minimum wage get you? Minimum living. This doesn't mean a new phone, a meal at a restaurant with friends, or a beer pub night. It is purely money to survive. Here's the kicker: if a person makes double the minimum wage, they can still only live off of the minimum wage. The only exceptions to this are when they have a life-happens situation, or if they have inflated their lifestyle: buying more stuff, taking on loans, or spending money on other non-essential ventures. This additional spending may be beneficial (like a gym subscription)! But whether this is "worth the money" depends entirely on you and you alone. And this is where meaning is lost in translation.

Is a house worth £100,000? Well, it depends. If it's in London then that's a bargain; if it's in the countryside, then maybe; and if it's about to be eaten into the ocean due to cliff erosion, then definitely not. It all depends on the context and perspective. Different things are worth different amounts to different people. One person may want to relax in the country while another wants to feel the rush of the city. A house in a city will have different intangible value to those two people. But money alone does not show that.

And this applies to hourly rates too. Take this example: Let's say you make £100 an hour, and you pay a cleaner £20 an hour for doing your house chores. With one hour of your work you bought 5 hours of someone else's time. That's how I like to think whenever I am thinking about making a purchase: How many hours would I need to work to afford X? Or better yet, "what else could I buy with this money?" Ask yourself: do I spend £20 on that thing I like, or do I save it for my vacation trip and increase the progress on that by 2%? Bit by bit you can make a fortune. Half the success of having money is to just not spend it stupidly.

## Conclusion

For me, success doesn't mean a lot of money. It means reasonable spending. I enjoy the process of saving money and not being a sucker by buying something useless to me. I always follow the 1/10 rule and I always think before I buy. This alone will make me well-off.
